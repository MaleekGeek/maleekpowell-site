A lot of us treat sleep like the thing we cut first. Up late finishing work. Up early before everybody else. "I'll sleep when it's done."

But your brain is busy while you're out. Scientists are still working out exactly how, and what they've found so far is worth knowing.

## The wash cycle

Your brain makes waste while it works, the same way your body does. One of those waste products is a protein called beta-amyloid. When too much of it builds up, it's linked to Alzheimer's disease.

In 2013, a team studying mice found that during sleep, the spaces between brain cells opened up by about 60%. Fluid could move through more easily, and beta-amyloid was cleared faster than when the mice were awake.

In 2019, another team watched 13 young adults sleep inside a brain scanner. During deep sleep, slow brain waves were followed by big, steady waves of fluid flowing in and out of the brain. It was the first time anyone had seen that in sleeping people.

## What happens when you skip it

In 2018, researchers kept 20 healthy adults awake for a whole night, about 31 hours straight. After that one night, beta-amyloid in parts of their brain had gone up by about 5%. The people with the biggest rise also reported the worst moods.

That was one night, in a small group, and nobody knows yet whether a good night's sleep afterwards undoes it. But it shows your brain notices even one missed night.

## The honest part

The science isn't settled. In 2024, a team in London tested this in mice a different way and found the opposite: the brain cleared waste more slowly during sleep, not faster. Other scientists have questioned how they measured it, and the argument is still going.

So I won't tell you sleep "detoxes" your brain like it's a fact. What I can tell you is that even one missed night shows up in the brain and in your mood. And the CDC says adults need at least seven hours a night.

## How to get more of it

These come straight from the CDC's sleep advice:

- Go to bed and get up at the same time every day, weekends too.
- Keep your room dark, quiet and a comfortable temperature.
- Take the TV, laptop and phone out of the bedroom if you can.
- Skip big meals, caffeine and alcohol close to bedtime.
- Move during the day. Even a short walk helps.

If you've tried all of that and you still can't sleep, talk to your doctor. Some sleep problems need more than good habits.

You're allowed to go to bed. The work will still be there in the morning.
